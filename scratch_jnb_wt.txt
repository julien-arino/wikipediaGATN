'''O. R. Tambo International Airport''' {{Airport codes|JNB|FAOR}} is an [[international airport]] serving the twin cities of [[Johannesburg]] and the main [[capital of South Africa]], [[Pretoria]]. It is situated in [[Kempton Park, Gauteng|Kempton Park]], [[Gauteng]]. It serves as the primary airport for domestic and international travel for South Africa and since 2020, it is Africa's [[List of busiest airports in Africa|second busiest airport]], with a capacity to handle up to 28 million passengers annually.<ref>{{Cite web|title=World Cup improvements at Johannesburg airport nearly complete|url=https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MZyAAUZrNLU|access-date=2023-01-09|website=www.youtube.com |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170406103619/https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MZyAAUZrNLU 
|archive-date=April 6, 2017 |date=April 5, 2010}}</ref> The airport serves as the hub for [[South African Airways]].

The airport was originally known as '''[[Jan Smuts]] International Airport''',<ref name=BOOK_IGTSA>{{cite book| last = Bulpin| first = T.V.| title = Illustrated Guide to Southern Africa| publisher = [[Reader's Digest]]| editor1-last = Mayhew| editor1-first = Vic| editor2-last = Duncan| editor2-first = Tony| editor3-last = Handler| editor3-first = Rosemund| editor3-link=Rosemund Handler | year = 1980| edition = 2| isbn = 0-620-04650-3| at = p. 198, col. 1}}</ref> after the former South African [[Prime Minister of South Africa|Prime Minister]]. It was renamed '''Johannesburg International Airport''' in 1994, and  subsequently on 27 October 2006 the airport was renamed after anti-apartheid politician [[Oliver Tambo]] (1917–1993).<ref>{{cite web|title=T Mbeki: OR Tambo International Airport renaming {{!}} South African Government|url=https://www.gov.za/t-mbeki-o-r-tambo-international-airport-renaming|access-date=2021-08-11|website=www.gov.za}}</ref> Its ICAO code was changed from FAJS to FAOR on 10 January 2013.

==History==
The airport was founded in 1952 as [[Jan Smuts]] International Airport, two years after Smuts's death. Situated near the town of [[Kempton Park, Gauteng|Kempton Park]] on the [[East Rand, Gauteng|East Rand]], it replaced [[Palmietfontein Airport|Palmietfontein International Airport]], which had handled European flights since 1945.

In 1943, a decision was made by the Cabinet of the [[Union of South Africa]] to construct three international airports with a Civil Airports Advisory Committee formed to investigate and report on the viability.<ref name="Great01">{{cite journal|last1=Greathead|first1=Walter Robinson|last2=Hawkins|first2=Robert Drake|title=First stages in the construction of the Jan Smuts Airport, Johannesburg - Historical|journal=Civil Engineering = Siviele Ingenieurswese|date=January 1948|volume=46|issue=1|pages=224–225|url=https://journals.co.za/content/civeng/46/1/AJA10212019_15004?fromSearch=true|access-date=18 March 2018|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180318183754/https://journals.co.za/content/civeng/46/1/AJA10212019_15004?fromSearch=true|archive-date=18 March 2018|url-status=live}}|{{subscription required|via=Sabinet}}</ref>{{rp|224}} That report was submitted to the Cabinet in March 1944 with one main international airport on the [[Witwatersrand]] and two smaller international airports at Cape Town and Durban.<ref name=Great01/>{{rp|224}} The South African Railways and Harbours Administration was given the role of managing the project and later in 1944, a member went to the United States to study standards and methods of construction.<ref name=Great01/>{{rp|224–5}} Four possible sites around Johannesburg were identified, with one south of Johannesburg chosen but soon discarded due to being situated on land with gold bearing reefs below.<ref name=Great01/>{{rp|225}} Sites were then narrowed down to Kempton Park and the existing airport at Palmietfontein.<ref name=Great01/>{{rp|225}}

Layouts and rough costing for the two sites were established and submitted for a ministerial decision.<ref name=Great01/>{{rp|226}} The site would be at Kempton Park and be named Jan Smuts Airport.<ref name=Great01/>{{rp|226}} The area outside Kempton Park was an expropriated undulating dairy farm of 3,706 acres with a 598 acre [[eucalyptus]] plantation.<ref name=Great01/>{{rp|227}} Sitting on a plateau, the area sloped away towards the east.<ref name=Great01/>{{rp|227}} The area was drained by the Blesbok River.<ref name=Great01/>{{rp|227}} The airport became operational on 1 September 1953.<ref name="FI01">{{Cite journal|date=2 October 1953|title=South Africa's Main Airport|url=https://archive.org/details/sim_flight-international_1953-10-02_64_2332/page/n71/mode/2up?q=Jan+Smuts|journal=Flight International|volume=64|issue=2332|page=481|via=Internet Archive}}</ref> The new airport was officially opened by Minister for Transport, [[Paul Sauer]] on 4 October 1953 having taken eight years to build at £6.2 million.<ref name=FI01/><ref name="For">{{Cite journal|date=16 November 1953|title=South Africa Officially Opens Jan Smuts Airport|url=https://archive.org/details/sim_international-commerce_1953-11-16_50_20/page/22/mode/2up?q=%22jan+smuts+airport%22|journal=Foreign Commerce Weekly|volume=50|issue=20|page=23|via=Internet Archive}}</ref> It had one main runway of 3,200m and two smaller ones of 2,514m that crossed the main with all runways being 60m wide.<ref name=FI01/><ref name=For/> A 1,000 men had been employed in the repair workshops.<ref name=FI01/> The technical areas consisted of 2,957m of roads, 26,477sqm of concrete apron while the hangars had openings of 106m at a height of 21m.<ref name=FI01/> It was expecting to manage thirty flights-a-day and over 200,000 passengers that year.<ref name=For/> Airlines using the airport at its opening were [[BOAC]], [[Air France]], [[KLM]], [[South African Airways|SAA]], [[Central African Airways]], [[Qantas]], [[El Al]], [[SAS Group]] and [[LAM Mozambique Airlines|DETA]].<ref name=FI01/>

In the late 1950s, jet passenger aircraft became the norm and there was a need to expand the existing ground facilities at the airport, which began in the 1960s and early-1970s. In addition to the new airside facilities, ground developments included: improved road access, parking areas, hotel, retail areas and car hire.<ref name="Mal01">{{cite journal|last1=Mitchell|first1=Malcolm|title=Chapter 9 : Airport infrastructure development in South Africa - a rapidly growing sector of the transport 'family': infrastructure|journal=Civil Engineering = Siviele Ingenieurswese|date=October 2014|volume=22|issue=9|pages=49–51|url=https://journals.co.za/content/civeng/22/9/EJC161417?fromSearch=true|access-date=18 March 2018|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180318183558/https://journals.co.za/content/civeng/22/9/EJC161417?fromSearch=true|archive-date=18 March 2018|url-status=live}}|{{subscription required|via=Sabinet}}</ref>{{rp|50}}

The late 1960s saw a new choice of aircraft for South African Airways, the Boeing 747.<ref name="West01">{{cite journal|last1=Westwood|first1=A.R.|last2=Klintworth|first2=K.E.|last3=Kemp|first3=W.J.|title=The Boeing 747 hangar at Jan Smuts Airport|journal=Civil Engineering = Siviele Ingenieurswese|date=October 1971|volume=13|issue=10|pages=339–348|url=https://journals.co.za/content/civeng/13/10/AJA10212019_19077?fromSearch=true|access-date=18 March 2018|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180318183728/https://journals.co.za/content/civeng/13/10/AJA10212019_19077?fromSearch=true|archive-date=18 March 2018|url-status=live}}|{{subscription required|via=Sabinet}}</ref>{{rp|339}} A decision was made by the Minister of Transport to obtain three, later five 747s for the airline.<ref name=West01/>{{rp|339}} Delivery would begin in October 1971 with the first flight to [[London]] on 10 December 1971 with daily services from February 1972.<ref name=West01/>{{rp|339}} These purchases however required new hangar facilities with the contract awarded in September 1969 initially worth R2,983,408.<ref name=West01/>{{rp|339}} Construction started in December 1968 and was completed in October 1971 for R8,000,000 while other work at the airport associated with the arrival of these new aircraft brought the costs to R40,000,000.<ref name=West01/>{{rp|341}} Other new buildings such as workshops, testing facilities, stores, staff accommodation and air cargo handling building were built. The new hangar would allow for two 747s in each bay with dimensions of 73.2&nbsp;m wide, 24.4&nbsp;m high and a depth of 91.4&nbsp;m.<ref name=West01/>{{rp|341}}

It was used as a test airport for [[Concorde]] during the 1970s, to determine how the aircraft would perform while taking off and landing at high elevations ('hot and high' testing).<ref>{{Cite web|title=CONCORDE SST : TIMELINE -70's|url=http://www.concordesst.com/history/70s.html|access-date=2023-01-09|website=www.concordesst.com |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20100511083330/http://www.concordesst.com/history/70s.html |archive-date=11 May 2010 |url-status=live}}</ref> During the 1980s, many countries stopped trading with South Africa because of the [[1961 in South Africa|United Nation sanctions]] imposed against South Africa in the struggle against [[History of South Africa in the apartheid era|apartheid]], and many international airlines stopped flying to the airport. These sanctions also resulted in South African Airways being refused rights to fly over most African countries, and in addition, the risk of flying over some African countries was emphasised by the shooting down of two passenger aircraft over [[Rhodesia]] (e.g. [[Air Rhodesia Flight 825]] and [[Air Rhodesia Flight 827|827]]),<ref>{{cite web|url=http://home.iprimus.com.au/rob_rickards/viscounts/crewpax.htm|title=Details p of Air Rhodesia Flight RH825|access-date=12 November 2006|work=Viscount Disasters|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20120318075013/http://home.iprimus.com.au/rob_rickards/viscounts/crewpax.htm|archive-date=18 March 2012|url-status=live}}</ref> forcing them to fly around the "bulge" of Africa. This required specially-modified aircraft like the long-range [[Boeing 747SP]]. A second runway was built at the airport in the late 1980s.<ref name=Mal01/>{{rp|50}}

In December 1993, a R120,000,000 upgrade at the airport was completed.<ref name=Air01/>{{rp|14}} The main part of the projects was an 880&nbsp;m, 3000&nbsp;t steel airside corridor consisting of two levels high of 6&nbsp;m wide with thirteen passenger bridges.<ref name="Air01">{{cite journal|title=Airside corridor facilitates passenger handling at Jan Smuts|journal=Civil Engineering = Siviele Ingenieurswese|date=December 1993|volume=1|issue=11|pages=14–16|url=https://journals.co.za/content/civeng/1/11/AJA10212019_17273?fromSearch=true|access-date=18 March 2018|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20180318182953/https://journals.co.za/content/civeng/1/11/AJA10212019_17273?fromSearch=true|archive-date=18 March 2018|url-status=live}}|{{subscription required|via=Sabinet}}</ref>{{rp|14}} The upper levels are connected to the departure lounges through security screening points. Lower levels are for arrivals for entry into the immigration and custom areas.<ref name=Air01/>{{rp|14}} A future provision for extensions to this airside corridor was included in the design.<ref name=Air01/>{{rp|14}} A new airside bus terminal was also added for bussing in passengers to aircraft not able dock next to the terminal. Other parts of the project included upgrading the terminal facilities for the passengers.<ref name=Air01/>{{rp|14}}

Following the ending of apartheid, the airport's name, and that of other [[international airport]]s in South Africa, were changed and these restrictions were lifted. With the creation of the [[Airports Company of South Africa]] (ACSA) in the mid-nineties, a plan to commercialise the airport began with new passenger and retail and airside facilities to handle a larger number of aircraft completing this phase in 2004.<ref name=Mal01/>{{rp|50}}

The airport overtook [[Cairo International Airport]] in 1996 as the busiest airport in Africa<ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.pprune.org/archive/index.php/t-124152.html |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20091013113358/http://www.pprune.org/archive/index.php/t-124152.html |title=Busiest Airports in Africa |website=Pprune.org |publisher=PRRuNe Forums |date=13 October 2009 |archive-date=2009-10-13}}</ref> and is the fourth-busiest airport in the Africa–Middle East region after [[Dubai International Airport]], [[Hamad International Airport]], and [[Abu Dhabi International Airport]]. In fiscal year 2010, the airport handled 8.82&nbsp;million departing passengers.<ref>{{citation |url=http://www.acsa.co.za/Tools/Documents/DocumentDownload.asp?FileID=7154 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20110720011058/http://www.acsa.co.za/Tools/Documents/DocumentDownload.asp?FileID=7154 |archive-date=2011-07-20 |title=Airports Company South Africa Annual Report – Part I }}</ref>

In late 2005, a proposal was made for the airport to be renamed "O. R. Tambo International", after former [[African National Congress]] President and anti-[[History of South Africa in the apartheid era|apartheid]] activist [[Oliver Tambo]], in a change to the policy of neutrally-named airports. The proposal was formally announced in the [[Government Gazette of South Africa]] on 30 June 2006, allowing a 30-day window for the public to register objections. The name change was implemented on 27 October 2006 with the unveiling of new signs at the airport. Critics noted the expense involved in renaming the airport. Corne Mulder of the [[Freedom Front Plus]] described the renaming as "nothing less than political opportunism and attempts by the ANC government to dodge the true socio-economic issues of the country".<ref>{{cite news |first=Riaan |last=Wolmarans |title=Welcome to OR Tambo Airport |url=http://www.mg.co.za/articlePage.aspx?articleid=276844&area=/breaking_news/breaking_news__national/ |publisher=Mail&Guardian |date=10 July 2006 |access-date=22 September 2006 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20060717042803/http://www.mg.co.za/articlepage.aspx?area=%2Fbreaking_news%2Fbreaking_news__national%2F&articleid=276844 |archive-date=17 July 2006}}</ref>

On 26 November 2006, the airport became the first in Africa to host the [[Airbus A380]].<ref>{{Cite news|title=Oliver R Tambo (Johannesburg) International Airport (JNB/FAJS)|url=https://www.airport-technology.com/projects/johannesburg/|access-date=2023-01-09|website=Airport Technology|language=en-US |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20100128055754/http://www.airport-technology.com/projects/johannesburg/ |archive-date=28 January 2010}}</ref> The aircraft landed in Johannesburg on its way to Sydney via the [[South Pole]] on a test flight.

There was no provision for rapid train access until 2010, when the [[Gautrain]] opened and allowed passengers to reach the airport from the Johannesburg CBD, Sandton and Pretoria.<ref name=Mal01/>{{rp|50}}

==Airport information==
[[File:O.R Tambo Bust.jpg|thumb|247x247px|A now removed O.R Tambo bust at the aircraft viewing deck above the CTB]]

O. R. Tambo International Airport is a [[hot and high]] airport. Situated 1,700 metres (5,500 feet) [[above mean sea level]], the air is thin.<ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.southafrica-travel.net/climate/eklima2.htm |title=Climate and weather in Johannesburg and on the Highveld, South Africa |publisher=Southafrica-travel.net |access-date=11 May 2013 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20120831091821/http://www.southafrica-travel.net/climate/eklima2.htm |archive-date=31 August 2012 |url-status=live }}</ref> This is the reason for the long runways.

On 10 January 2013 the airport's ICAO code was changed from FAJS to FAOR.<ref>{{Cite web|url=http://www.caa.co.za/resource%20center/AIP%27s/2012/S087-12%20FAJS-FAOR.pdf|title=South African Civil Aviation Authority, AIRAC AIP Supplement S087/12, 20 September 2012}}{{Dead link|date=November 2023 |bot=InternetArchiveBot |fix-attempted=yes }}</ref>

=== South African Airways Museum ===
The South African Airways Museum once was located at the airport. This room full of South African Airways memorabilia was started by two fans of the airline as a temporary location until they could set it up in one of Jan Smuts International's buildings in 1987. The museum has since relocated to [[Rand Airport]] (FAGM).

=== Aircraft viewing decks ===
The airport has two viewing decks. One is located above the Central Terminal Building, and the other in an administrative section of the airport above the international check-in counters. There are regular displays of Oliver Reginald Tambo, the airport's namesake in the viewing decks.

Another decommissioned runway was 14/32 (which crossed runways 03L/21R and 03R/21L); it was converted into taxiway Echo.

The runways are equipped with [[approach lighting system]]s. Sequenced flashers are not used at any South African runways and therefore not installed. Touchdown zone (TDZ) lighting is available, but never turned on. Runway Threshold, Edge and Centerline lights are the only lighting available. During busy periods, outbound flights use the western runway (03L/21R) for take-off, while inbound flights use the eastern runway (03R/21L) for landing. Wind factors may cause numerous variations, but on most days flights will take off to the north and land from the south.

=== Taxiways and aprons ===
O. R. Tambo International Airport has a network of asphalt taxiways connecting runways, aprons and maintenance facilities.<ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.caa.co.za/Aeronautical%20Charts/O.R.%20Tambo%20INTL%20-%20FAOR/FAOR__AERODROME%20GROUND%20MOVEMENT%20CHART_AD-02_04%20FEB%202016.pdf|title=FAOR Ground Movement chart|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20160324183900/http://www.caa.co.za/Aeronautical%20Charts/O.R.%20Tambo%20INTL%20-%20FAOR/FAOR__AERODROME%20GROUND%20MOVEMENT%20CHART_AD-02_04%20FEB%202016.pdf|archive-date=24 March 2016|url-status=live}}</ref> All of these taxiways are 30.5 metres wide, except for taxiways Echo and Juliet which are 60 metres wide; they were formerly runways 14/32 and 09/27, respectively. The airport also has nine aprons. Cargo aircraft park at aprons Golf and Whiskey. Many airlines have their aircraft wait long hours between arriving and departing flights. Such aircraft and other cargo aircraft are parked at aprons Delta and Foxtrot to free up jetbridges. Aprons Alpha, Charlie and Echo have jetbridges that connect them to their respective gates. The Bravo apron is not connected to the terminal building, and thus aircraft that are parked there must use an airport bus service.

==Developments==
ACSA reported that major new developments took place at the airport, in preparation for the [[2010 FIFA World Cup]].
The development includes expansion of the international terminal, with the new international pier (opened in 2009), which includes gates for the [[Airbus A380]] and increased capacity at the same time.<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://aecom.com/projects/o-r-tambo-international-airport/|title=O.R. Tambo International Airport|access-date=2025-02-08|website=aecom}}</ref>
A new Central Terminal building, designed by Osmond Lange Architects and Planners, was completed on 1 April 2009.{{Citation needed|date=April 2017}} An additional multi-storey [[Parking|parkade]] was built in January 2010, at a cost of R470 million opposite the Central Terminal Building,{{citation needed|date=March 2018}} plus Terminal A was also upgraded and the associated roadways realigned to accommodate more International Departures space.

The Central Terminal Building (CTB) (cost: [[South African rand|R]]2 billion) boosted passenger capacity at the landside of the terminal in 3 levels, and allows direct access from international and domestic terminals. Additional luggage carousels were added on 12 March 2010 to accommodate the [[Airbus A380]].{{citation needed|date=March 2018}} Arrivals are accommodated on Level 1, with departures expanded on Level 3; Level 2 accommodates further retail and commercial activities. The [[Gautrain]] [[Rapid transit|Rapid Rail Link]] station is above the terminal.

The new International Pier (cost: R535 million) has increased international arrivals and departures capacity in a two-storey structure with nine additional airside contact stands, four of which are Airbus A380 compatible.{{Citation needed|date=April 2017}} [[Jetway|Air bridges]] are already in place and the existing [[Duty-free shop|duty-free mall]] will be extended into this area. Additional lounges and passenger-holding areas will be constructed on the upper level.

In March 2024, a capital investment plan was announced that shall encompass over $1 billion in several airports across South Africa. Significant upgrades to passenger and cargo facilities is touted for OR Tambo with a new cargo terminal and new passenger terminal to be developed second between the two runways, six additional bussing gates at the current terminal, and various other changes.{{Citation needed|date=April 2024}}

==Terminals==
[[File:A collection of aircraft at Johannesburg OR Tambo International Airport.jpg|thumb|A number of different aircraft at the international terminal]]

There are six [[Airport terminal|terminals]] at the airport, in three major areas: the international terminals; the domestic terminals; and the transit terminals. The transit terminal housed disused parts of the old domestic terminals. It has been mostly demolished to build a new Central Terminal that will provide an indoor link between domestic and international terminals, as well as a central passenger check-in area and more gates. It was constructed for the [[2010 FIFA World Cup]]. Terminals A1 and A2 handle international passengers while the other two terminals handle domestic passengers. Due to the airport's design, departure and arrivals terminals are considered separate terminals. The Central Terminal that is under construction will be named Terminal A3 and it will be used for both international and domestic passengers.

The two terminals, Terminal A and Terminal B, have been restructured. Several airlines now use Terminal B for all check-ins, for both national and international flights. The airlines that use Terminal B include Air Cote D'Ivoire, RwandAir, South African Airways, Airlink, Lift-Airline, FlySafair, KLM, Air France, Ethiopian Airlines, Qantas and Air Mauritius.<ref>{{cite web | url=https://www.airport-johannesburg.com/jnb-or-tambo-departures-terminal-B-domestic?tp=12 | title=Johannesburg or Tambo Airport (JNB) - Terminal B - Domestic Departures - Today (12:00h to 17:59h) }}</ref>

The ample parking available at O. R. Tambo International Airport was revamped as part of the upgrades made prior to the 2010 World Cup with the introduction of state-of-the-art technology that allows visitors to identify available parking spaces easily.{{citation needed|date=June 2024}}

==Ground transport==
[[File:Gautrain..., O R Tambo Intl Airport South Africa.jpg|thumb|[[Gautrain]] at O. R. Tambo International Airport, with direct pedestrian link to the terminal]]

===Rail transit===
The [[Gautrain]], a modern regional express rail system, serves the airport thanks to its station located directly in the terminal. It links the airport with [[Sandton, Gauteng|Sandton]], a major [[Central business district|business district]] and a primary tourist area, and, from there, the rest of the Gautrain system. The Gautrain is generally praised for its safety and overall efficiency.<ref>{{cite web |title=The Gautrain stays on track |url=https://midrandreporter.co.za/296181/the-gautrain-stays-on-track/ |date=28 November 2021|access-date=23 September 2023}}</ref>

===Car===
The airport is easily accessible by car and it is located 20 kilometres northeast of [[Johannesburg CBD|Johannesburg Central]]<ref>{{Cite web |title=257 Commissioner St to Isando |url=https://www.google.com/maps/dir/-26.2030597,28.0578552/-26.1312918,28.2259115/@-26.1774403,27.9395482,11z/data=!4m2!4m1!3e0?entry=ttu&g_ep=EgoyMDI1MTAyMi4wIKXMDSoASAFQAw== |access-date=2025-10-26 |website=[[Google Maps]] |language=en}}</ref> at the eastern end of the [[R24 (South Africa)|R24 Airport Freeway]]. It can be accessed by the R24 Airport Freeway (also known as the Albertina Sisulu Freeway) from Johannesburg Central to the west and the [[R21 (Gauteng)|R21 highway]] from [[Pretoria]] to the north and the central part of the [[East Rand]] to the south. The R24 intersects with the R21 near the airport and with the O. R. Tambo Airport Highway. This highway goes through the airport terminals, separating them from the parking bays, but it branches off into two directions: "departures" and "arrivals", and then it re-branches into the intersection.

===Bus===
Five bus city lines, operated by Metrobus and Putco, pass through the airport twice a day. The buses are accessible in the morning and the evening, when there are many passengers departing and arriving. There are also private bus lines operating express buses to the CBD of Johannesburg, as well as other locations.

==Accidents and incidents==
* 20 October 1957 – A [[Vickers Viscount]] G-AOYF, operated by [[Vickers]] on a test flight, was damaged beyond economic repair when the starboard undercarriage collapsed following a heavy landing.<ref name=ASN201057>{{cite web |url=http://aviation-safety.net/database/record.php?id=19571020-0 |title=Accident description |publisher=Aviation Safety Network |access-date=8 September 2009 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20121025075542/http://aviation-safety.net/database/record.php?id=19571020-0 |archive-date=25 October 2012 |url-status=live }}</ref>
* 22 September 1972 – A [[Beech 18]] operated by ''United Air'' (not to be confused with [[United Airlines]] of the United States or [[United Airways]] of Bangladesh) crashed 12&nbsp;km (7.4m) N of JNB while attempting to land back at Johannesburg. All 3 occupants on board were killed along with two persons on the ground.<ref>{{ASN accident|id=19720922-0|title=ZS-PRA|access-date= October 13, 2023}}</ref>
* 1 March 1988 – A [[Comair (South Africa)|Comair]] [[Embraer EMB 110 Bandeirante]] ZS-LGP operating [[Comair Flight 206|Flight 206]], exploded in mid-air whilst on final approach. All seventeen occupants were killed. A passenger was suspected of detonating an explosive device but to this day it has never been proven.<ref>{{cite web |url=http://aviation-safety.net/database/record.php?id=19880301-0 |title=ASN Aircraft accident Embraer 110P1 Bandeirante ZS-LGP Germiston, c. 13 km SW of Johannesburg International Airport (JNB) |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20121012025149/http://aviation-safety.net/database/record.php?id=19880301-0 |archive-date=12 October 2012}}</ref>
* 22 April 1999 – [[Boeing 727|Boeing 727 ZS-IJE]] was damaged beyond repair by large hailstones while on approach for landing. The aircraft landed safely with no loss of life.<ref name="SACAA-J1027070">{{cite web|url=http://www.caa.co.za/resource%20center/accidents%20&%20incid/reports/1999/j10_2_7070.pdf|title=Boeing B727-23 (sic), Johannesburg International Airport|publisher=[[South African Civil Aviation Authority]]|access-date=4 November 2009|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20081207224657/http://www.caa.co.za/resource%20center/accidents%20%26%20incid/reports/1999/j10_2_7070.pdf|archive-date=7 December 2008|url-status=live}}</ref>
* 3 November 2001 – A [[Reims-Cessna F406]] crashed shortly after takeoff from runway 03R, killing all three occupants. The aircraft did not have a valid certificate of airworthiness at the time of the incident.<ref name="SACAA-7426">{{cite web|url=http://www.caa.co.za/resource%20center/accidents%20&%20incid/reports/2001/7426.pdf|title=ReimsF406 – 700m South of the threshold of Runway 03R FAJS|publisher=[[South African Civil Aviation Authority]]|access-date=4 November 2009|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20081207063922/http://www.caa.co.za/resource%20center/accidents%20%26%20incid/reports/2001/7426.pdf|archive-date=7 December 2008|url-status=live}}</ref>
* 9 April 2004 – An [[Emirates (airline)|Emirates]] [[Airbus A340-300]] A6-ERN operating flight EK764 from Johannesburg to Dubai sustained serious damage during takeoff when it failed to become airborne before the end of the runway, striking 25 [[Approach lighting system|approach lights]], causing four tyres to burst which in turn threw debris into various parts of the aircraft, ultimately damaging the [[Flap (aircraft)|flap]] drive mechanism. This rendered the flaps immoveable in the takeoff position. The aircraft returned for an emergency landing during which the normal braking system failed as a result of the damage. The aircraft was brought to a stop only 250 metres from the end of the 3,400-metre runway using reverse thrust and the alternate braking system.<ref>{{cite web|url=http://avherald.com/h?article=3dd14563&opt=0 |title=Report: Emirates A340 at Johannesburg on April 20th 2004, runway overrun during takeoff, near catastrophy |publisher=Avherald.com |access-date=2022-05-01}}</ref><ref>{{cite web |url=http://aviation-safety.net/database/record.php?id=20040409-0 |title=ASN Aircraft accident Airbus A340 A6-ERN |publisher=Aviation Safety Network |access-date=18 December 2011 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20090325061009/http://aviation-safety.net/database/record.php?id=20040409-0 |archive-date=25 March 2009 |url-status=live }}</ref> In their report, South African investigators found that the captain had used an erroneous take-off technique, and criticised Emirates training and rostering practices.<ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.flightglobal.com/news/articles/emirates-censured-in-airbus-a340-johannesburg-runway-overrun-203980/ |title=Emirates censured in Airbus A340 Johannesburg runway overrun probe |author=David Learmount |date=11 January 2006 |publisher=Flightglobal |access-date=18 December 2011 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20120130184820/http://www.flightglobal.com/news/articles/emirates-censured-in-airbus-a340-johannesburg-runway-overrun-203980/ |archive-date=30 January 2012 |url-status=live }}</ref> This incident was similar [[Emirates Flight 407|to an incident which happened at Melbourne airport in 2009]].
* 22 December 2013 – A [[British Airways]] [[Boeing 747-400]] G-BNLL operating flight BA33 collided with a building at the airport. Four ground-handling staff in the building sustained minor injuries. The airplane was written off and scrapped by April 2015.<ref>{{cite web |url=http://avherald.com/h?article=46d6e18c&opt=0 |title=Accident: British Airways B744 at Johannesburg on Dec 22nd 2013, took wrong taxiway and buried wing in building |access-date=19 September 2018 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20171017182303/http://www.avherald.com/h?article=46d6e18c&opt=0 |archive-date=17 October 2017 |url-status=live }}</ref><ref>{{cite news | url=https://www.telegraph.co.uk/news/worldnews/africaandindianocean/southafrica/10534084/British-Airways-plane-collides-with-building-at-Johannesburg-airport.html | location=London | work=The Daily Telegraph | first=Bonnie | last=Malkin | title=British Airways plane collides with building at Johannesburg airport | date=22 December 2013 | access-date=3 April 2018 | archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20131223225530/http://www.telegraph.co.uk/news/worldnews/africaandindianocean/southafrica/10534084/British-Airways-plane-collides-with-building-at-Johannesburg-airport.html | archive-date=23 December 2013 | url-status=live }}</ref>
* 26 October 2015 – A [[British Airways]] operated by [[Comair (South Africa)|Comair]] [[Boeing 737-400]] ZS-OAA operating flight BA6234 from [[Port Elizabeth]] suffered a gear collapse while landing at the airport. There were no injuries.<ref>{{cite web |url=http://avherald.com/h?article=48e5e437&opt=0 |title=Accident: Comair B734 at Johannesburg on Oct 26th 2015, left main gear collapse on landing |access-date=19 September 2018 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170419013627/http://avherald.com/h?article=48e5e437&opt=0 |archive-date=19 April 2017 |url-status=live }}</ref><ref name="EWN">{{cite news |last1=Kubheka |first1=Thando |title=British Airways Plane in 'Incident' at OR Tambo |url=http://ewn.co.za/2015/10/26/Comair-plane-crash-lands-at-OR-Tambo |access-date=9 March 2017 |agency=Eyewitness News |date=26 October 2015 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170629172000/http://ewn.co.za/2015/10/26/Comair-plane-crash-lands-at-OR-Tambo |archive-date=29 June 2017 |url-status=live }}</ref>
